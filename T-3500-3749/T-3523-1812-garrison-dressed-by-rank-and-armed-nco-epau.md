1812 garrison dressed by rank and armed: NCO epaulettes, musicians' red coats and drums, fatigue dress, officers' epaulettes by rank and sashes, muskets with bayonets carried by duty.

**Acceptance:** (state it before working — the definition of done, never weakened to pass)

The owner, 2026-11-08, with an Ogden-style plate of the U.S. Army about 1812 beside his view of the fort: *"and all the men soldiers have the same hats? Dont the soldiers have different appearance based on rank? and none of them appear to have firearms?"*

Scope: the Fort Dearborn garrison of 14 August 1812 (Heald's company of the First Infantry and its four officers), on dev, seen at normal distance (3-30 m) on desktop and a 390x780 phone.

- Rank reads on the figure, from the 1812 dress research (docs/RESEARCH/people-1812-3461/dress.json) and its sources, each piece graded and under a liberty: sergeants two worsted epaulettes, corporals one on the right shoulder; drummers and fifers in the musicians' red coats faced blue, the drummers with their drums and the fifers with fife cases; officers by grade (captain's epaulette on the right, lieutenant's and ensign's on the left), crimson sashes for the line officers on duty, the surgeon's mate in his black collar and black plume; officers keep chapeau and sword.
- Undress/fatigue: men at chores (gardens, stables, bake house, armourer, the fatigue party, the cornfield) wear the issued fatigue dress (linen shirt or frock, waistcoat, linen trousers) without coat, cap or belts; no later forage cap.
- Enlisted men on duty are armed with a musket and fixed bayonet of the 1795 Springfield / Charleville pattern, carried by duty: sentries at shoulder arms, the guard's sergeant and corporal at order arms, men walking outside the pickets with arms slung; men at chores unarmed, their arms piled where the work is outside the fort.
- The card's provenance line says what each man wears and carries.

Fort Dearborn Massacre monument (1893-1931) at 18th Street and Calumet, beside the Pullman house, built to its photographs in 1904.

## What it is

Carl Rohl-Smith's bronze group *The Fort Dearborn Massacre* ("Black Partridge saving Mrs. Helm"), commissioned by George M. Pullman and set up in 1893 on Pullman's 18th Street frontage, near where the battle of 15 August 1812 was believed to have been fought. Five figures on a rocky ground: the attacking warrior with a raised tomahawk (left), Margaret Helm, Black Partridge stepping in behind her with one hand raised and a musket, the prostrate post surgeon (Van Voorhis) underfoot, and a child reaching out (right front). The group stands on a dark granite pedestal: a two-step stage base, a plinth, a die with a bronze bas-relief panel on each face (the front panel shows the column leaving the fort), and a moulded cap under the rock. Lettered on the die below the front panel: THE FORT DEARBORN MASSACRE / AUGUST 15, 1812 / BLACK PARTRIDGE SAVING MRS. HELM.

**Numbers to build from** (each with its source; cross-check against the photographs before trusting one):
- Group about 9 ft (2.7 m) high on a 10 ft (3.0 m) pedestal, 19 ft overall; pedestal of dark Quincy granite; cast by the Henry Bonnard Bronze Co. (Inter Ocean, 15 September 1892, quoted on Chicagology; library `cgn-1729-inter-ocean-1892-history-in-bronze-headline`).
- Stage-like base 8 ft by 9 ft, 5 ft deep (WBEZ, "The missing monument"). Statue "about nine feet" (Wikipedia).
- Four bas-reliefs on the pedestal's panels (Rand, McNally guide, 1893, quoted on Chicagology's Prairie Avenue page).

## Where it stood (1904 scene)

On the **north side of 18th Street at its east end, by Calumet Avenue**, on the walk in front of the Pullman estate's ashlar 18th Street wall (square coped piers, a lamp standard), with the rock-faced end of the Pullman stable behind it to the west. The photographs settle this:
- Detroit Publishing LC-D4-12552 (`loc_det_4a08066_pullman_residence`, c. 1900): taken on 18th Street west of Calumet looking west-north-west; the monument's pedestal is the right foreground (about x 740 px). **This camera is already solved** (`data/reference_views/pullman_house_1729_prairie/detroit_det_4a08066.json`), so the monument can be placed through it first.
- Detroit Publishing LC-D4-12551 (`loc_det_4a08065_pullman_residence`, 1893-1901; camera solved by T-1880): from the south-west corner of Prairie and 18th, the monument at the far right end of 18th Street. A second, independent bearing.
- Barnes-Crosby ICHi-19208 ("at Calumet Street and 18th", c. 1900).
- Text conflict, recorded here: the 1893 Rand, McNally guide puts it "at the foot of Eighteenth Street"; the 1905 *Views of Chicago* caption and later texts say "at Eighteenth Street and Prairie Avenue", and a 1929 Tribune "a few feet from Prairie avenue on 18th street". The photographs place it by Calumet; follow them and write the disagreement into the structure record's note.

## Which periods

- **1904: yes.** Stood here from 1893 until 1931 (the Rand, McNally 1893 guide already describes it; one Art Institute note reads a Chicago Historical Society plaque as "1894-1931", so the dedication year may be 1893 or 1894 — either way it stands in 1904).
- **1812 and 1835: no.** It postdates both. (The 1812 scene's battle ground is a separate matter and the fighting is not staged, AGENTS.md standing constraint.)
- **Later history, for the card only** (no scene covers it): moved in 1931 to the Chicago Historical Society's lobby (Pullman's will had left it to the Society in trust for the city); subtitled "The Potawatomi Rescue" in 1972; returned to the Prairie Avenue district (the Clarke House grounds) in the 1970s or 1980s (sources disagree); removed from public view in 1997 after long criticism from American Indian activists, and in city storage since. The 2009 Battle of Fort Dearborn Park at 18th and Calumet has no statue. If a modern scene is ever added, the monument is absent from it.

## Owner's ruling on accuracy (Kevin, 2026-11-12, verbatim)

> you should be accurate to the photos it must be accurate do not change native depictions as done in the monument, this must accurately represent the monument

So this is a **faithful replica of the monument as photographed**, binding on whoever builds it:
- **Every figure as Rohl-Smith made it**, read from the photographs: the poses, the anatomy, the dress and undress, the headdresses and feathers, the hair, the tomahawk, the musket, the faces and expressions, the woman's drapery, the fallen surgeon, the child, and the rocky ground. **Do not soften, re-dress, re-pose, idealise or "correct" the Native figures**, and do not apply the project's Native dress, variation or likeness rules (`dress_1812_native.json`, the 1812 card rules) to them: they are a bronze sculpture of 1893, not people in the scene.
- **No generic stand-ins.** The portable-human rigs may serve only as an armature; the finished group has to read as this sculpture in every photograph, figure by figure, not as five posed town figures.
- The pedestal, the steps and chamfers, the die, the bronze panels (including what the front relief shows), the inscription's wording and layout, the cap, and the dark bronze on dark granite, all to the photographs.
- Accuracy is not traded for budget: if the group needs more triangles than the Light tier allows at close range, raise the budget (AGENTS.md: "just raise it", never silently) or give it distance LODs, but the near view stays exact.
- Where no photograph shows a part (the far side of the group, the rear and side panels), build it from the Inter Ocean 1892 rear sketch and the other views, mark it `reconstructed`, and record it in `docs/LIBERTIES.md`. Nothing is invented where a picture shows it.
- The card states the monument's history factually: who commissioned and made it, where it stood, the 1931 move, and that it was taken off public view in 1997 after long objection from Native groups. That is history about the object; it does not change the object. Keep `review_required: true` with its own reason (a depiction of the 1812 battle and of Potawatomi people), as the standing constraint requires; the flag changes nothing in the model and does not block release.
